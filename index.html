What this segment shows

Six companies are attempting healthcare-specific AI governance as their core product. None are building a cross-institutional standard. Signal 1 is the most complete platform in this segment: AI intake and review, an AI registry (single source of truth for all AI in use), post-deployment monitoring with risk-tiered observability and automated alerts, agent management, and portfolio impact dashboards. Customers include NewYork-Presbyterian, Mount Sinai Health System (seven hospital campuses, Icahn School of Medicine), Inova, and Trillium Health Partners (Canada). Two of those four are major AMCs, which puts Signal 1 inside the exact institutional profile the VAMOS Collaborative targets. 37 employees on $10M raised ($50M post-money). Mount Sinai's CDTO described the problem Signal 1 solves as monitoring "performance, safety, and impact at scale" across imaging, generative AI, and agent-based systems. That is nearly identical to the problem statement VigilAI uses. Signal 1 is not waiting for a standard to form. They are selling now, into AMCs, against the same pain point, with a working product. Ferrum Health is the most mature company in this segment by funding ($37M) and deployed footprint (Sutter Health, Allina Health, Carle Health, 45M+ lives across 400+ care sites). Their Observability Lens tracks live AUROC, drift, and safety checks, which is close to continuous algorithmovigilance. CEO is a physician (Pelu Tran, MD, Stanford, co-founded Augmedix). But Ferrum is also a deployment platform (Model Hub, Deployment Fabric), which blurs their positioning. Vitea is the other strong direct competitor (continuous monitoring, governance workflows, Hartford HealthCare and Mercyhealth contracts, CEO who built and exited Jvion). ALIGNMT is compliance and assessment-first with CHAI certification. Onboard is pre-deployment vetting. Polysight is regulatory intelligence. The weakness shared by every company in this segment is the same: proprietary product, no open standard, no multi-institutional governance, no cross-site benchmarking, no peer-reviewed intellectual framework. VigilAI is the only entry that covers all nine capabilities.

What this segment shows

These are the "why can't we just use what we already have?" answers. Qualified Health ($255M, backed by NEA and Anthropic) is the largest company on this map, but it's an AI deployment platform with monitoring as a feature, not an independent monitoring standard. Its governance covers models deployed through its own platform, not a vendor-agnostic standard for all algorithms. Penguin AI ($25M, Snowflake Ventures) is a model-creation platform whose CEO talks publicly about governance as a strategic priority, but has not declared independent model monitoring as a product. As a model creator, Penguin self-monitoring its own algorithms would create an inherent conflict of interest, the same structural problem that makes independent pharmacovigilance necessary in drug safety. Its models would themselves be subject to VAMOS monitoring at any institution running both. Epic Seismometer is open-source and healthcare-specific, but does point-in-time validation, not continuous monitoring. Agent Factory (2027) will govern Epic-built agents but not third-party models. IBM watsonx.governance has real monitoring and drift detection capabilities (it's one of the stronger horizontal enterprise tools), but it's a generic platform with no clinical governance layer. Oracle Health is building AI-first EHR features with embedded governance, but like Epic, it monitors its own ecosystem, not a vendor-agnostic standard. Collaborative members running Oracle/Cerner should expect governance features to emerge as platform-native capabilities over the next 12 to 18 months. The same structural limitation applies to all of them: a vendor monitoring its own AI is not an independent standard. None produce cross-institutional benchmarks or standardized algorithmovigilance.

Reading the chart

The gold VigilAI shape is the only one that reaches the outer ring on every axis. Signal 1 comes closest: strong on monitoring, governance, and drift, but drops to zero on open methodology, cross-site benchmarking, and community governance. That's the structural gap no proprietary product can close. Qualified Health scores low on monitoring and benchmarking because governance is a platform feature, not its core product. Fiddler scores high on drift detection but drops to near-zero on clinical governance and healthcare specificity. Epic Seismometer has open methodology but no continuous monitoring or governance workflows.

Why the gap is structurally defensible

Filling the entire radar requires two things venture capital cannot buy: institutional legitimacy (AMCs co-governing through executed LOIs) and intellectual provenance (Peter Embi's peer-reviewed algorithmovigilance framework). Qualified Health has raised $255M (PitchBook-verified) and still cannot convene a governance coalition or claim to have originated the field. Vitea's CEO built and exited Jvion for 9 figures and still can't replicate the Collaborative. Those are structural moats.
